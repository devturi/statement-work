There are seven realistic ways to get a bank statement PDF into Excel: retype it, copy and paste, use Excel's own PDF import, export from a PDF editor, use a generic online converter, download transactions from your bank instead, or use a converter built for bank statements. The right one depends on how many statements you have, whether they're scans, and how much checking you can afford.

## First: is your PDF digital or scanned?

This decides which methods are even possible. Open the PDF and try to select a single word.

- **You can highlight and copy text:** it's a *digital* PDF, generated by the bank. Every method below can work.
- **The whole page selects as one block, or nothing selects:** it's a *scan* (or photo) inside a PDF. There's no text, only an image. Only methods with OCR will work. See [why scanned statements are hard to convert](/guides/why-scanned-bank-statements-are-hard-to-convert/).

## Method 1: Retype it

Open the PDF on one screen and Excel on the other, and type.

- **Good for:** one short statement, when nothing else is to hand.
- **Problems:** slow, tiring and error-prone. Transposed digits (`86.40` typed as `68.40`) are the classic mistake, and they don't show until the balance doesn't tie out.
- **Handles scans:** yes, since you are the OCR.

If you must, type the running balance too and add a check column, `previous balance + amount`, so each typo shows up on the row where it happened.

## Method 2: Copy and paste

Select the transaction area in your PDF viewer, copy, paste into Excel.

- **Good for:** short, simple digital statements.
- **Problems:** PDF text has no real columns, so pasted data often lands in one column or splits unpredictably. Wrapped descriptions become extra rows. Separate money-in and money-out columns shift. Headers and footers from each page come along.
- **Handles scans:** no.

Excel's **Text to Columns** and **Paste Special → Text** can rescue some pastes. Expect cleanup on every page.

## Method 3: Excel's built-in PDF import

Some versions of Excel can import tables from a PDF directly (in Microsoft 365 for Windows, look under **Data → Get Data → From File → From PDF**). It detects tables on each page and loads them through Power Query.

- **Good for:** digital statements with clearly ruled tables.
- **Problems:** each page often comes in as a separate table that you have to append. Detection varies by layout. Multi-line descriptions and split amount columns still need fixing. Availability depends on your Excel version and platform.
- **Handles scans:** no; it needs a digital PDF.

## Method 4: Export from a PDF editor

Full PDF editors such as Adobe Acrobat (paid versions) can export a PDF to an Excel workbook, and some include OCR for scanned documents.

- **Good for:** occasional conversions if you already pay for the software.
- **Problems:** general-purpose exports try to reproduce the *page*, not the *transactions*. Expect merged cells, headers repeated per page, and amounts spread across columns that need consolidating.
- **Handles scans:** with OCR, sometimes. Results vary with scan quality.

## Method 5: A generic online PDF-to-Excel converter

Many websites convert any PDF to Excel.

- **Good for:** quick one-offs with non-sensitive documents.
- **Problems:** they aren't built for statements, so the output usually needs the same cleanup as an editor export. More importantly, you're uploading a sensitive document. Check where it's processed, how long it's kept and whether it's used for anything else before you upload a bank statement. Our [security checklist](/guides/is-it-safe-to-upload-bank-statements-online/) helps.
- **Handles scans:** some do, with OCR.

## Method 6: Don't convert, download

Many banks let you download recent transactions from online banking as CSV or another data format.

- **Good for:** your own accounts, recent periods.
- **Problems:** downloads often cover only a limited history, so older months may exist only as PDF statements. Columns and date formats differ between banks. And if a client sent you PDFs, you don't have their online banking.
- **Handles scans:** not applicable.

If this covers what you need, it's the cleanest option. Use it.

## Method 7: A converter built for bank statements

Dedicated tools such as statement.work read a statement as a statement: they know what a transaction row, a running balance and a page header are.

- **Good for:** regular work, many statements, several banks, scans and photos.
- **Problems:** a sensitive upload, so judge the tool's data handling (statement.work encrypts in transit and at rest, deletes source files after processing on a retention window you control, and never trains on your data). Like any extraction, check the result against the statement.
- **Handles scans:** yes, along with phone photos.

The output is one row per transaction with date, description, amount and running balance in consistent columns, plus a category, exported to Excel, CSV or JSON.

| Generic online converter | Medium | Sometimes | No | Data handling, cleanup |
| Bank download | Low | n/a | No | Limited history |

## Whatever you choose, check it

Every method can drop or misread a line. Three checks catch nearly everything:

1. **Balance check:** opening balance + money in − money out = closing balance.
2. **Count check:** number of rows equals the number of transactions on the statement.
3. **Running balance chain:** each balance equals the previous balance plus that row's amount. The first broken link points at the problem row.

## Which method should you use?

- **One digital statement, once:** copy-paste or Excel's PDF import.
- **Your own recent transactions:** the bank's download.
- **Scans or photos:** OCR, either a PDF editor with OCR or a statement converter.
- **Regular work across clients and banks:** a statement converter, for the consistent columns as much as the speed.
