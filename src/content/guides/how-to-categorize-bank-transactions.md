To categorize bank transactions well, use a short, fixed category list, apply rules based on the payee and transaction type, and treat transfers, refunds and owner money separately from income and expenses. Consistency matters more than detail: a category used the same way every month is worth more than a precise one used differently each time.

## Why categories matter

A list of transactions answers "what happened". Categories answer "where did the money go?" They're the basis of:

- profit and loss figures and budgets,
- tax returns and the evidence behind them,
- spotting cost creep in subscriptions, fees and suppliers,
- answering client questions without reading every line.

## Start with a fixed list

The most common mistake is inventing categories as you go. Three months later there's "Software", "Subscriptions", "SaaS" and "Apps", and none of the totals mean anything. Write the list first, keep it short, and add to it deliberately.

## A sample category list for a small business

This is a starting point, not tax or accounting advice. Your accountant's chart of accounts, and the rules where you are, come first.

| Group | Category | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Income | Sales / customer receipts | Invoice payments, card processor payouts |
| Income | Other income | Interest received, cashback (check treatment) |
| Cost of sales | Materials and stock | Supplier purchases for resale |
| Cost of sales | Subcontractors | Freelancers delivering client work |
| Operating | Rent and premises | Office rent, utilities, service charges |
| Operating | Payroll | Salaries, wages |
| Operating | Payroll taxes and pensions | Employer contributions, payroll tax payments |
| Operating | Software and subscriptions | SaaS tools, hosting, licenses |
| Operating | Equipment | Computers, tools (may be capital items) |
| Operating | Travel | Train, flights, hotels, mileage reimbursements |
| Operating | Meals and entertainment | Client meals (rules vary) |
| Operating | Marketing and advertising | Ads, sponsorships, design |
| Operating | Professional fees | Accountant, lawyer, consultants |
| Operating | Insurance | Business insurance premiums |
| Operating | Phone and internet | Mobile, broadband |
| Operating | Office supplies | Stationery, postage |
| Finance | Bank fees | Monthly fees, transaction charges, FX fees |
| Finance | Interest paid | Loan and overdraft interest |
| Finance | Loan repayments | Principal repayments (not an expense) |
| Tax | Tax payments | Sales tax/VAT, income or corporation tax |
| Non-P&L | Transfers between own accounts | Checking to savings, card payments |
| Non-P&L | Owner draws / contributions | Money taken out or put in by owners |
| Non-P&L | Refunds | Money back from a supplier |
| Review | Uncategorized | Anything you can't place yet |

## Rules for the tricky items

### Transfers between your own accounts

Moving money from checking to savings, or paying a credit card from the bank account, is **not** income or spending. Categorize both sides as transfers. Counting a card payment as an expense *and* its card purchases as expenses double-counts everything.

### Refunds

A refund from a supplier reduces that expense; it isn't income. Either categorize it as the original expense category (as a negative) or use a refunds category and net it off later. Pick one approach and stick with it.

### Batched payouts

A card processor deposit is many sales minus fees. Record the gross sales and the fee separately if you need accurate figures; otherwise you'll understate both income and costs.

### Loan payments

The interest is an expense; the principal repayment isn't. If the statement shows one amount, split it using the lender's statement.

### Owner money

In small businesses, owners often pay personal costs from the business account or put money in. Those are draws and contributions, not expenses or income.

### Mixed personal and business accounts

Sole traders who use one account for everything need a "personal" category so business totals stay clean.

## Use the clues on the statement

- **Payee name:** the strongest signal. The same payee nearly always gets the same category.
- **Transaction type:** direct debits and standing orders are usually recurring bills; card payments are day-to-day spending; incoming transfers are often customer payments.
- **Amount pattern:** a fixed amount on the same day each month is a subscription or rent.
- **Timing:** quarterly payments are often taxes or insurance.

## Make it repeatable

1. **Keep a payee → category list.** Once a payee is decided, it's decided.
2. **Categorize as you go,** monthly, while you still remember what things were.
3. **Review the uncategorized bucket** before closing a month, never leaving it to year-end.
4. **Check totals for sense:** a category that's suddenly zero or doubled usually means a rule changed.

## Mapping to a chart of accounts

Your accounting software's chart of accounts is the authoritative list. Categories from a bank statement tool are a working layer. Keep a simple mapping table (tool category → account code) so imports land in the right accounts. Review it when you add a category on either side.

## Summary

Fix the list, write rules for transfers, refunds and owner money, and lean on payee and transaction type. With consistent categories, reconciliation, reporting and tax prep all get faster.
